# Fact sheet (researched November 2026)

Use only these facts. "SAFE WORDING" lines should be used close to verbatim. Items marked DO NOT PRINT must stay out.

## Basics and reference numbers
- 1 unit of electricity = 1 kilowatt-hour (kWh): a 1,000-watt appliance running for one hour. kW (kilowatt) measures size or power of a plant; kWh measures energy produced or used. A plant's size is quoted in kW (often written kWp).
- Generation: about 4–5 units per kW per day on a clear day in most of India (roughly 1,200–1,500 units per kW per year). Andhra Pradesh planning figure: about 4 units per kW per day, about 120 units per kW per month.
- Roof area: about 10 square metres (100–120 sq ft) of shadow-free area per kW.
- Panels: 25-year power-output (performance) warranty and at least 10-year product warranty are required by MNRE for modules under central schemes; inverters at least 8 years. SAFE WORDING: "25-year performance warranty; design life of 25 years or more."
- Emissions: India's grid average is about 0.68 kg of CO₂ per unit (Central Electricity Authority CO₂ Baseline Database v22, FY 2025-26). So 1 kW of solar (~1,400–1,500 units/year) avoids about 1 tonne of CO₂ a year. DO NOT PRINT tree equivalents.
- How an on-grid system behaves in a power cut: grid-tied inverters must switch off automatically when the grid fails ("anti-islanding"), so no power is sent into lines that linemen may be repairing. So a plain on-grid system gives no power in a power cut. A hybrid system (with battery) keeps chosen loads running, isolated from the grid. Off-grid systems are not connected to the DISCOM at all.

## India green-energy context
- India's installed solar capacity: about 174 GW (October 2026, MNRE). Total installed power capacity about 560 GW; non-fossil sources about 308 GW, around 55% (CEA, Sept 2026).
- Non-fossil sources crossed 50% of installed capacity in June 2025, five years ahead of the 2030 pledge.
- Targets: 500 GW of non-fossil capacity by 2030 (this includes large hydro and nuclear; do not call it "500 GW of renewables"); net-zero emissions by 2070.
- India ranks 3rd in the world in total renewable energy capacity (IRENA 2026). DO NOT PRINT "3rd in solar".
- "Green energy" for these purposes: electricity from sources that are naturally replenished and emit little or no CO₂ in operation — solar, wind, hydro, biomass, and storage or green hydrogen charged from them.

## Metering
- Net metering: one bidirectional meter records import and export; units are netted; you pay the retail tariff on net import. Surplus is carried forward as credit or paid at a rate the State Electricity Regulatory Commission (SERC) decides.
- National rule (Electricity (Rights of Consumers) Rules 2020, amended 2021): where a state has no provision of its own, net metering is for loads up to 500 kW or the sanctioned load, whichever is lower; larger loads get net billing / net feed-in; gross metering is optional. The 500 kW figure is a default — each state's regulation sets the real limits. SAFE WORDING: "Net-metering limits, settlement periods and surplus rates are set by each State Electricity Regulatory Commission."
- 2024 amendment: rooftop applications up to 10 kW are deemed accepted without a technical feasibility study; for others the study must be done within 15 days.
- Gross metering: all solar generation is exported and sold to the DISCOM at a feed-in tariff fixed by the SERC, usually for the agreement term; the building's own consumption is billed separately at the normal retail tariff. Two readings: a solar generation meter and the usual consumption meter. Typically suits large roofs with little daytime use, investors, and systems above net-metering limits.
- Net billing (also called net feed-in): like net metering in wiring — solar serves the building first, surplus is exported — but settled in money, not units. Imports are priced at the retail tariff, exports at the (lower) feed-in tariff, and one is subtracted from the other.
- Group net metering: surplus from one consumer's plant is adjusted against that same consumer's other electricity connections in the same DISCOM area (e.g. a trust with several buildings, a company with a warehouse and an office).
- Virtual net metering: one shared plant's output is credited to several different consumers in agreed shares (e.g. residents of an apartment block, who each have no roof of their own). Both exist only where the state regulation allows them. Group and virtual net-metered rooftop systems can qualify for the PM Surya Ghar subsidy where the DISCOM permits them.
- Feed-in tariff: the per-unit rate paid for solar power sent to the grid, set by the SERC.
- Sanctioned load / contract demand: the maximum load your connection is approved for; plant size is normally capped by it.

## Behind the meter / front of the meter
- Behind-the-meter (BTM): generation or storage on the consumer's side of the utility meter, serving the consumer's own load first. Examples: rooftop solar on a home or factory, a home or commercial battery, a "zero-export" plant fitted with a reverse-power relay so nothing flows to the grid.
- Front-of-the-meter (FTM): plants that feed the grid directly — utility-scale solar parks, open-access plants, plants under a power purchase agreement with a DISCOM.
- Why BTM matters: every unit used directly avoids buying a unit at the retail tariff and avoids network losses; no wheeling or transmission charges are involved.
- Andhra Pradesh (regulator's practice directions, Aug 2026): behind-the-meter captive systems with zero grid injection need prior intimation to the DISCOM rather than a full approval process. SAFE WORDING: "In Andhra Pradesh, zero-export behind-the-meter systems follow a simplified intimation process."

## Andhra Pradesh snapshot (as of November 2026)
- Regulator: APERC (Andhra Pradesh Electricity Regulatory Commission). DISCOMs: APEPDCL (Eastern, Visakhapatnam), APCPDCL (Central, Vijayawada), APSPDCL (Southern, Tirupati).
- Rooftop regulation: APERC Regulation 4 of 2023, amended by Regulation 10 of 2025.
- Net metering: new systems up to 500 kW. Gross metering: available for larger systems (up to 5 MW). Single-phase connections: up to 5 kW, within sanctioned load.
- Settlement: monthly. If you import more than you export in the month, the net is billed at the retail tariff; if you export more, the surplus is paid at the feed-in tariff fixed by APERC. DO NOT PRINT a ₹ figure for the AP surplus rate.
- Domestic consumers up to 10 kW under PM Surya Ghar: no application fee or net-meter cost, feasibility study waived.
- Green open access: APERC Regulation 3 of 2024 (amended 2025). Banking allowed within the monthly billing cycle up to 30% of monthly consumption, with a banking charge of 8% in kind. Under the AP Integrated Clean Energy Policy 2024, cross-subsidy surcharge and additional surcharge do not apply to captive consumption. DO NOT PRINT any "10-year waiver of open access charges".
- Always add: "Confirm current rules with your DISCOM before you decide."

## Open access
- Definition: the right, under the Electricity Act 2003, to use the transmission and distribution network to buy power from a seller other than your local DISCOM, or to carry power from your own plant located elsewhere, by paying regulated charges.
- Physical vs contractual: electrons are pooled on the grid; you draw from the nearest source. Contractually, the units the generator injects are metered and credited against the units you draw, after deducting losses.
- Parties: generator; transmission company (state/central transmission utility); the DISCOM, which carries ("wheels") the power to you and remains your backup supplier; the State Load Despatch Centre (SLDC), which schedules and accounts for it; the consumer.
- Green Energy Open Access Rules 2022 (central, amended 2023): open to consumers with contract demand or sanctioned load of 100 kW or more (one connection, or several in the same DISCOM electricity division adding up to 100 kW); no minimum for captive consumers. Before this the threshold was generally 1 MW. Applications go through a national single-window portal (greenopenaccess.in), run by Grid-India, and are processed by state nodal agencies. Rules call for a decision in 15 days; DO NOT promise a timeline. SAFE WORDING: "National rules and state regulations allow consumers with 100 kW or more of sanctioned load to buy green power through open access, on terms set by each state."
- Three routes:
  - Captive: you own the plant (alone). Tests: captive users hold at least 26% of ownership and consume at least 51% of the electricity generated each year.
  - Group captive: several consumers together hold at least 26% and together consume at least 51%, in proportion to their shareholding; a developer holds the rest and builds/runs the plant. (Captive rules were updated by the Electricity (Amendment) Rules 2026, effective April 2026: group companies can count as one captive user.)
  - Third-party sale: you simply buy power from an independent generator under a power purchase agreement, with no ownership.
  - Captive and group captive consumption is exempt from cross-subsidy surcharge and additional surcharge. It still pays transmission and wheeling charges, losses, banking and scheduling charges. DO NOT PRINT "captive users pay no grid charges". Third-party sale pays all charges.
- Charges, one line each:
  - Transmission charge — for using the high-voltage grid.
  - Wheeling charge — for using the DISCOM's local distribution network.
  - Losses — a percentage of energy deducted in kind for what is lost in the wires.
  - Cross-subsidy surcharge (CSS) — compensates the DISCOM, because large consumers' tariffs help subsidise other categories.
  - Additional surcharge — covers the DISCOM's fixed costs for power it had already contracted for you.
  - Banking charge — the fee for "storing" surplus energy with the DISCOM, usually taken in kind.
  - Scheduling (SLDC) charges — for scheduling, metering and accounts.
  - Electricity duty — a state tax on consumption.
  - Standby charge — for backup supply from the DISCOM if your source fails.
  All values are set state by state.
- Energy banking: midday surplus is "deposited" with the DISCOM and drawn later in the same billing cycle, less a banking charge in kind. Under the national rules, banking is allowed at least monthly, up to at least 30% of the consumer's monthly consumption from the DISCOM; unused banked energy is generally not carried forward. Terms vary by state.
- Green tariff: a consumer who cannot do open access can ask the DISCOM to supply green power at a "green tariff" set by the SERC.
- Inter-state transmission charge waiver for solar/wind is being phased out for new plants and does not apply to power within one state. Best left out of the brochure.
- Who it suits: SAFE WORDING: "Typically viable for consumers with large, steady daytime demand." Savings: SAFE WORDING: "Savings depend on state charges, voltage level and consumption pattern; ask for a site-specific estimate." Contracts: "long-term agreements, often 10 years or more." DO NOT PRINT a ₹/unit savings figure.

## Other green-energy terms (glossary material)
- DISCOM: distribution company that supplies and bills your electricity. SERC: State Electricity Regulatory Commission, which sets tariffs and rules. MNRE: Ministry of New and Renewable Energy.
- Renewable Consumption Obligation (RCO): a legal requirement on designated large consumers (including captive and open-access users) and DISCOMs to meet a minimum share of their electricity from renewables; it replaces the older Renewable Purchase Obligation (RPO) and rises each year towards about 43% by 2029-30.
- Renewable Energy Certificate (REC): a tradable certificate representing 1 MWh (1,000 units) of renewable electricity, bought on power exchanges to meet obligations or green claims. I-REC: an international voluntary equivalent, used mainly by multinational companies.
- Virtual PPA: a financial contract in which a company supports a renewable plant and receives its green certificates without physically receiving the power; CERC issued guidelines in December 2025.
- Green power is also traded on power exchanges (green day-ahead and term-ahead markets).
- PPA (power purchase agreement): long-term contract to buy electricity at an agreed per-unit rate.
- ALMM: the government's Approved List of Models and Manufacturers for solar panels. DCR (domestic content requirement): subsidy-linked rule that panels (and, from June 2026, their cells) be made in India.

## Ownership models (industry-standard definitions; say "typically")
- CAPEX: the customer pays for and owns the system, keeps all the savings, can claim depreciation if a business, and is responsible for upkeep (usually via an annual maintenance contract).
- OPEX / RESCO (Renewable Energy Service Company): a developer or investor pays for, owns and runs the plant on the customer's roof or land; the customer pays only for the units it uses, at an agreed per-unit rate under a PPA, typically lower than the grid tariff; the developer handles operation and maintenance. Rooftop PPAs for businesses typically run 15–25 years.
- Lease: the customer pays a fixed monthly rent for the system; the lessor owns it.
- BOOT (build-own-operate-transfer): like RESCO, but ownership passes to the customer at the end of the term.
- Investor view: under RESCO, the investor's income is the per-unit payments from the power user over the contract; key risks are the user's ability to pay, plant performance and regulation. DO NOT PRINT any return or IRR percentage.

## Paying for it: subsidy, loans, tax
- PM Surya Ghar: Muft Bijli Yojana (central scheme for home rooftop solar; currently sanctioned until March 2027; target 1 crore households; over 65 lakh homes covered by Nov 2026). Central subsidy for individual homes: ₹30,000 per kW for the first 2 kW, ₹18,000 for the third kW; maximum ₹78,000 for 3 kW or more (1 kW ₹30,000; 2 kW ₹60,000; 3 kW and above ₹78,000). Housing societies / resident welfare associations: ₹18,000 per kW for common facilities including EV charging, up to 500 kW (at 3 kW per house). Conditions: residential connection, grid-connected rooftop system, made-in-India panels from the government's approved list, installation by a registered vendor. Apply only on the national portal pmsuryaghar.gov.in; the subsidy is paid into the applicant's bank account after the plant is commissioned. Subsidy is for homes only — not for commercial or industrial connections.
- DO NOT PRINT any Andhra Pradesh state top-up subsidy.
- Home solar loans under the scheme: public-sector banks offer collateral-free loans for systems up to 3 kW (up to about ₹2 lakh), tenure up to 10 years; larger loans for systems up to 10 kW. SAFE WORDING: "at a concessional interest rate linked to the RBI repo rate; ask your bank for the current rate." Loan application is linked from the national portal through JanSamarth (jansamarth.in). DO NOT PRINT an interest rate or margin percentage.
- Priority sector: under RBI's priority sector lending directions (2025), renewable-energy loans to individual households count up to ₹10 lakh per borrower, and loans to renewable power generators count up to ₹35 crore.
- Business financing, typically: term loans from banks and NBFCs; IREDA (a government renewable-energy lender); SIDBI rooftop-solar loans for MSMEs; credit-guarantee cover (CGTMSE) for micro and small enterprises, whose ceiling is ₹10 crore from April 2025. Lenders typically fund about 70–80% of project cost. Or no loan at all, through the OPEX/RESCO route.
- Tax for businesses: solar plants fall in the renewable-energy block eligible for depreciation at 40% a year on written-down value under current income-tax rules (half that if used under 180 days in the first year). SAFE WORDING: "Ask your chartered accountant how it applies to you." DO NOT PRINT a section number or "additional depreciation".
- GST: solar panels and solar power generating systems are taxed at 5% (reduced from 12% in September 2025). For a turnkey supply-and-install contract, 70% of the value is treated as goods at 5% and 30% as services at 18%, about 8.9% overall.
- PM-KUSUM (farm solar: solar pumps and small plants on farmland): the first phase closed to new applications in March 2026; a successor scheme is awaited. Mention only briefly, if at all.
- Costs and payback: DO NOT PRINT a cost per kW. SAFE WORDING for payback: "Payback is often around 3 to 6 years, depending on your tariff, how much of the power you use yourself, and any subsidy."
